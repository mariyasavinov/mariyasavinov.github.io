Mechanics in development

How cells collectively leverage mechanical asymmetries and feedback to drive developmental processes robustly remains poorly understood. One critical developmental process is neurulation: the formation of the neural tube, which later develops into the central nervous system. Together with the Mangan group and the Munro lab, I am using data-driven modeling to investigate the mechanochemical feedbacks which drive neural tube closure.

Vertebrate ectodermal appendages (teeth, hair follicles, scales, feathers, mammary glands) all originate from interactions between two adjacent tissue layers: the epithelium and mesenchyme. One of the first fundamental bifurcations during ectodermal appendage organogenesis relates to the physical bending of epithelial sheets, leading either to invagination or evagination. The mechanisms that determine epithelial bending direction remain poorly understood. I collaborate with the Sam Norris in the Shubin Lab to combine biophysical modeling with in vivo experiments in catsharks to determine the key mechanisms which dictate these divergent bending modes.

Bacterial aggregates: formation and microarchitecture

The bioluminescent bacterium, Vibrio fischeri, is the exclusive symbiont of the light organ of bobtail squids. In order to colonize the light organ of new squid generations, V. fischeri must form transient aggregates in a mucus-rich ciliated zone on the outer surface of the light organ. I collaborate with the Nirody Lab to investigate how the microenvironment mechanics impact and potentially drive bacterial aggregation.

Understanding how microarchitecture of biofilms impacts resource distribution can serve to inform mechanisms of addressing antibiotic resistance. I collaborate with the Nirody Lab to investigate, using an inverse problems perspective, how metabolic activity of P. aeruginosa biofilms depends on their underlying microarchitecture.

Actin network dynamics

The actin cytoskeleton—complex self-organized assemblies of actin protein filaments and auxiliary proteins—plays important roles in cell shape and mechanics through the dynamics of self-organized higher-order architectures such as networks. Through molecular-scale interactions with molecular motors, global cell-scale contractile behaviors emerge, harnessed for cellular processes such as cell organization, motility, and intracellular transport. Mathematical modeling in conjunction with in vitro reconstitutions can reveal the relative importance of active stresses, friction, cell geometry, and turnover in directing actin network dynamics. My collaborators on such work have included Alex Mogilner, Charles S. Peskin, Kinneret Keren, and the Cytomorpho Lab of Profs. Laurent Blanchoin and Manuel Théry.

Collective cell migration

Collective cell migration plays key roles in processes such as embryonic development and cancer metastasis. The ability of cell clusters to collectively migrate in a directional manner requires cues such as signaling in development.  However, how cells in cancer clusters coordinate their movements is still not well understood. Toward this aim, I have worked with the Carmofon Lab to reveal how mechanics of the ECM can drive the directed migration of tumor cell clusters.
